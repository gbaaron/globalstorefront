The mission

Starbucks has an app, mobile ordering and a rewards program. The bakery down the street has a Wix template and a Facebook page. We even that out.

Big chains have

  • Their own ordering platform
  • Loyalty and rewards
  • Real-time inventory
  • Customer data they own
  • A digital team

Now a local shop can too

  • Ordering on their own site
  • Loyalty built in
  • Live inventory
  • Their own customer list
  • Us
This is why the company exists. Say it and mean it — it lands harder than any feature.

05

The pitch

Start with a question

Never “I sell websites.” Be curious about their business.

Find the pain, then stop talking

“What's your biggest frustration with it?” The problem they name is your angle.

Plant the seed

One or two sentences tying what we do to that problem. No more.

Show the demo on your phone

A working site beats any description. Every time.

Ask what they'd want

“If we built this for you, which features matter most?”

Ask for the call

Fifteen minutes with the team. Get a number or an email. That's the close.

Questions you'll get

“That's a great one for the call with the team” is always a valid answer.

The most common question. Don't guess and don't give a range.

Say this

“It really depends on what features you need — a simple site versus one with ordering, a loyalty program, and a full admin dashboard are totally different projects. The team will give you an exact quote after a 15-minute call.”

If they push: “Let Aaron give you the real number — he's done enough of these that it's a ten-minute conversation.”
Say this

“Usually about a week — sometimes less depending on the project. You also get a private preview link while it's being built, so you can see it in real time and give feedback before anything goes live.”

Lead with this. Most agencies take weeks or months.
Say this

“For day-to-day stuff — yes. Updating your inventory, changing prices, managing orders — all of that is built into your dashboard and you handle it yourself. Tell us upfront what you want to be able to change easily, and we'll make sure it's in there. Deeper changes to the site's structure or design go through us.”

Be upfront: Global Storefront owns the code. They own their content, their data and their customers.
Say this

“Anything structural goes through us — it's $25 per half hour or $40 per hour. The team is always available. If you have a few things you want changed, it saves you money to do them in one session.”

Push the discovery call. That's where Aaron works out what to put in their dashboard so they rarely pay for changes.

Don't trash their site. Ask whether it does anything.

Say this

“Totally — most of our clients did too. The real question is: is it actually bringing in business, or is it more of an online business card that just exists? A custom site with ordering or booking built in is a tool that works for you 24 hours a day.”

Say this

“Not at all. You tell us how your business works — what your customers do, what would make your day easier — and the team builds around that. You just need to use it.”

Say this

“The team is always available. Fixes and changes are billed at $25 per half hour or $40 per hour — and if you have several things, it's worth bundling them. You're never left stranded.”

Frame it: a direct line to the person who built the site beats a broken plugin and nobody to call.

08

Objections

An objection is a question in disguise. Dig for the real one.

Don't argue and don't agree. Find out what they pay now.

Say this

“I get that. Can I ask what you're currently paying? Wix, Squarespace, any ordering apps, booking tools — it adds up more than people realize. The call gets you a real number to put next to that.”

Restaurants on DoorDash: “If you're doing $8,000 a month in delivery orders at 25%, that's $2,000 a month going to them.”
Store credit, used carefully: Aaron sometimes takes part of the cost in store credit — a pizza shop pays in pizza. Not a default. If cash is truly the only barrier: “It might be worth a conversation with Aaron directly — he's flexible with the right businesses.”
Say this

“That's fair. Just out of curiosity — is it actively bringing in new customers on its own, or is it more of an online business card that just exists?”

Still not moved? Offer the free build: “Would you be okay if we built one for you anyway, just so you can see it? No cost, no commitment.” Aaron will build a demo for their business at no charge. It is the strongest close on a skeptic.
Say this

“Totally understand. It's a 15-minute call and the team handles everything after that — you don't build anything yourself. When would be a better time for me to come back? Next week?”

Book a specific time. “I'll come back sometime” never happens.

There's usually one unspoken concern. Find it.

Say this

“Of course. What would make the decision easier — is it the price, or is there something specific you'd want to see first?”

If it's price: “The call is just to get you an exact quote. Fifteen minutes, no commitment.”
Say this

“Absolutely. Would it help to set up the call with both of you? That way all the questions get answered at once and neither of you has to explain it to the other.”

Be respectful. Don't trash the nephew.

Say this

“That's great — if they can build what you need, that works. The sites we build aren't just design — they have real backend systems like live ordering, inventory, dashboards. It's a more complex build than most people expect. If you ever want a second opinion on what it would take, the call is free.”
